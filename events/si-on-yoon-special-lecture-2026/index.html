Abstract

Communication is fundamental to everyday social interaction, yet the cognitive mechanisms underlying language processing during natural conversation remain poorly understood. In this talk, I will present a series of studies examining how speakers adapt their language to conversational partners and how memory and cognitive abilities support successful communication across healthy and clinical populations. First, using a novel experimental paradigm to study naturalistic multiparty conversation, I examine how speakers tailor their language to partners with different knowledge states. Findings demonstrate that speakers flexibly adapt their language to their partners’ knowledge, even when simultaneously communicating with multiple individuals. This work also explores how speakers balance competing informational needs in conversations involving three to seven individuals. Second, I investigate the role of memory in audience design by examining language production in individuals with hippocampal amnesia and healthy older adults. These studies reveal how memory representations support conversational interactions and the extent to which successful audience design relies on hippocampal-dependent memory processes. Finally, I extend this work to individuals with neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, to examine how changes in memory and cognition affect conversational language use. This research highlights both preserved abilities and potential challenges in adapting language to conversational partners. Together, these findings highlight the interplay between language, memory, and social cognition in natural conversation, advancing our understanding of successful communication and its implications for aging and neurodegenerative disorders.

연사 소개

Si On Yoon is an Associate Professor in the Department of Communicative Sciences and Disorders at New York University. She received her Ph.D. in Psychology from the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign in 2016. Her research investigates the cognitive mechanisms underlying natural conversation, with a particular focus on the role of memory in supporting language use and social communication in adults with and without neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease. Her research has been supported by the National Institutes of Health (NIH).
